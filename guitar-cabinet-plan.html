Master Measurement & Climate Plan: 8-Instrument DIY Guitar Cabinet

Revision 2 — engineering review and corrections applied. Verify all instrument dimensions yourself before cutting wood.

0. Read This First — Reality Check

This is an ambitious, achievable build, but four things are harder than they first appear. Address them deliberately or the climate goals will not hold:

1. A PAX is not a sealed box. The frame is melamine-faced particleboard with a thin fiberboard back and gapped door hinges. Achieving a true 45–50% RH "vault" depends almost entirely on your sealing work (Section 5), not on the cabinet itself.
2. Particleboard holds screws poorly. Eight instruments plus hardware is real weight hanging off the frame. Hang from a plywood backer/French cleat that spans to the solid frame members, never from raw particleboard, and anchor the whole cabinet to wall studs (anti-tip). See Section 3.
3. Heat rises — and your amps are on the bottom. The tube Orange Rocker 15 is the real heat source (the solid-state Crush Bass 50 runs much cooler). A hot tube amp under a sealed vault will push the vault temperature up. Plan for thermal isolation (Section 4) and accept that real temperature control mostly comes from the room's HVAC, not from inside the box.
4. A humidifier and a dehumidifier in one chamber will fight each other unless you give the controllers a dead zone between their setpoints (Section 6).
Every dimension below for your instruments is an estimate. Measure each instrument's true maximum body width, body depth (profile thickness), and overall length, and add your own neck/headstock width. The cumulative body-depth total is what fills the cabinet width, so measure depths carefully. Measure twice, cut once.

Hung edge-out, each headstock's ~3" width runs front-to-back along the 22" depth (not across the width), so headstocks do not clash side-to-side; the binding constraint is purely the cumulative body-depth sum vs. the internal width. Eight-across fits, but with no finger room to lift an instrument out without nudging its neighbor. Use a fix below to buy clearance.

Fixes (pick one or combine)

  • Relocate the Backpacker (easiest). It is small and light — move it to a dedicated wall hook or lay it flat on a small shelf. That returns ~3" to the row, turning zero margin into comfortable spacing for the other seven.
  • Use two depth ranks. With 22" of depth you can run a front rank and a rear rank of hangers. Put the thin solid-bodies (1.25–1.75") in the rear rank and the deep acoustics/oud in the front rank. This roughly halves the width crunch and gives generous access room. (Stagger the two ranks slightly in height so a rear instrument lifts out cleanly.)
  • Drop to 6–7 per row. If you'd rather keep one tidy row, leave the Oud and/or Backpacker out of the rack and display them separately.

Hanger hardware

  • Use proper yoke-style guitar hangers (auto-grip or felt-lined) screwed into a 3/4" plywood mounting board, not into the PAX particleboard. Run the board vertically/horizontally to the frame's solid members and through-bolt or use a French cleat.
  • For the oud, the bulbous back means a standard neck yoke leaves it hanging well proud of the wall — verify it clears neighbors and the cedar lining, and consider a wider cradle.
  • Pad every contact point with the cedar/cork — nitro-finished and vintage instruments (the Rickenbacker) can react with some foams; use felt or cured cork, not raw foam.

4. Thermal Isolation (Amp → Vault)

The amp lives directly under the vault, so manage the heat path:

  • Insulate the divider's underside. Add foil-faced foam board or a reflective radiant barrier to the bottom of the divider shelf to reject the amp's radiant heat. Leave a small air gap so the shelf underside isn't a hotplate.
  • Vent the amp zone to the room, not upward. The bottom section stays open at the front; that is the heat's exit. Keep the divider's seal continuous so warm air can't migrate up around the edges.
  • Give the tube Rocker the headroom. Tube amps vent heat upward, so the Rocker 15 (the shorter of the two) gets the most clearance to the divider — keep nothing stacked on top of it. The taller Crush Bass 50 is solid-state and runs cool, so its tighter clearance is fine.
  • Don't run the tube amp 24/7. The Rocker only heats the cabinet while powered. Power it down when not playing and the thermal load on the vault disappears (the solid-state bass amp adds little either way).
  • Be honest about temperature control. Inside a sealed wooden box you cannot meaningfully air-condition; the vault temperature will track the room. Real temperature stability comes from keeping the room at 68–75°F. The ITC-308 (Section 6) is best used to switch a small, thermostatically-limited warming element in a cold room, or simply as a high/low alarm — not as a cooler.

5. Sealing & Wood Buffering

  • Seal the weak points of the PAX: caulk the back-panel perimeter, reinforce or replace the thin fiberboard back if it flexes, and gasket the door. EPDM rubber gaskets around the door frame plus compression latches give an airtight seal; account for ~1/4" thickness reduction at the closure.
  • Spanish cedar lining (1/4" veneer on walls) buffers moisture, smells great, and deters insects. It reduces internal dimensions by ~1/2" total in width and depth — already accounted for in Section 3. Lining the back and sides is enough; you needn't line the door.
  • The cedar is also your safety net. Its hygroscopic mass slows RH swings, so during a power outage the vault drifts slowly rather than crashing. Pair it with a couple of two-way humidity packs (e.g. 49% Boveda) as passive backup.
  • Let everything off-gas first. New particleboard, silicone caulk, and EPDM all release VOCs/formaldehyde as they cure. Build, seal, run the fans, and air the vault out for 1–2 weeks before sealing instruments inside. Use neutral-cure (not acetoxy/vinegar-smell) silicone to avoid acid attack on metal hardware.

6. Climate Hardware & Control Logic

This cabinet uses a monitored + passive + manual approach — not an automated humidifier loop. A sealed, cedar-lined box has so much hygric mass that it drifts over days, not hours — so a passive buffer holds the baseline, a sensor alerts you to real drift, and you top up by hand during dry spells. Less to fail, no water running unattended, no leak risk, no mineral dust. (Prefer full automation? See the optional closed-loop fallback at the end of this section.)

Lighting detail — warm wash, door + override

  • Strip & quality: use a CRI 90+ (ideally 95) warm-white ~3000K strip — cheap CRI 80 strips make wood look flat and greenish; high-CRI is the single detail that makes the guitars look rich. Prefer a 24 V strip over 12 V for less voltage drop over the run.
  • Mount it as a wash, not dots: seat the strip in an aluminum channel with a frosted diffuser, hidden behind the front top rail (a valance) aiming down the guitar faces, so light rakes the instruments without glaring out through the glass at you. Optional short vertical strips down the inner side stiles for even fill.
  • Control = both: a magnetic reed door switch turns the light on automatically when you open the door (display-case behavior), wired to override a smart RF/WiFi single-colour dimmer that drives the strip the rest of the time. Door closed → the dimmer holds whatever you set (off, or a dim glow to admire through the glass); door open → full brightness regardless; close it and control returns to the dimmer.
  • Heat & seal: the aluminum channel heat-sinks the strip so it runs cool (a few watts — negligible against the climate). Its lead is one more thin low-voltage wire through the existing caulked grommet, not a new hole.

Manual humidity routine (how it actually runs day to day)

  1. Baseline: Boveda + cedar hold ~45–50% on their own most of the year. You do nothing.
  2. Alert low (dry spell): run the diffuser a while until it climbs back to ~48%, then put it away.
  3. Alert high (rare in a sealed box): crack the door, let it equilibrate, check the room; the Boveda packs absorb minor excess. Persistent high RH is the only reason you'd add the optional dehumidifier.
  4. Seasonal: replace Boveda packs when stiff; recalibrate the sensor once a year.

Optional: full-automation fallback

If you later decide you don't want to think about it at all, bolt on a closed loop without changing anything above: an Inkbird IHC-200 hygrostat (AC-outlet, ~$40) with its probe in the vault, switching a hidden ultrasonic mist-maker disc in a low reservoir in the amp zone (its 24 V adapter on the Inkbird's switched outlet), fogging up through the grommet. The disc mists whenever powered, so the Inkbird gates it cleanly. The monitor, Boveda, fans, and light all stay exactly as they are.

7. Power, Cable Management, Monitoring & Failover

Electrical layout

  • One mains hub. A single GFCI surge-protected power strip with a resettable breaker, mounted to the amp-zone back wall off the floor, feeds everything that's plugged in: both amps, the LED driver/dimmer, and the fans' USB adapter. Only one cord leaves the cabinet, exiting a grommet at the back to a GFCI wall receptacle.
  • Nothing runs unattended inside the vault. The monitor is a battery unit and the Boveda packs are passive — neither needs power or a wire. The manual diffuser is plugged in only while you're using it. The sole always-on vault items are the low-voltage fans and the LED strip, both fed from the amp zone.
  • Keep water below mains. When you run the manual diffuser, sit it in a shallow tray in the amp zone, lower than the strip and any outlet — water vessels never sit above electronics.

Cable management

  • One sealed pass-through, now nearly empty. Only two thin low-voltage leads cross into the vault — the LED strip lead and the fan leads — through a single caulked grommet in the divider shelf. (No humidifier cord, no sensor probe, no drain: the manual/passive approach removed them, so the seal is much easier.)
  • Drip loop on every cord. Let each cable dip below its entry point before reaching the grommet, so any condensation runs off the low point instead of tracking along the wire through the seal.
  • Corral cords up the back. Run an adhesive cable raceway (D-Line or similar) up the rear wall so the fan and LED leads stay off the instruments. Bundle with hook-and-loop ties, not zip ties.
  • Label both ends and leave a little service slack — you'll pull the fans/strip occasionally to clean or replace them.

Monitoring & failover

  • The monitor is the whole safety system. Set high/low alarms (RH < 40% or > 55%, temp > 80°F) that reach your phone when you're not home — the alert is what makes the manual approach safe. Salt-test calibrate (75% RH reference) before you rely on it; cheap hygrometers are often off by 5–10%. A cheap second hygrometer inside is worthwhile insurance for the vintage Rickenbacker.
  • Power loss is a non-event here. Cedar mass + Boveda hold RH passively for days; the fans and light simply go off, harmlessly. Nothing can fail on and flood or over-humidify, so no UPS is needed — at most, keep the monitor's batteries fresh so alerts survive an outage.

8. Suggested Build Sequence

  1. Assemble PAX frame; anchor it to wall studs (anti-tip) before loading anything.
  2. Install the 3/4" plywood divider; caulk all joints; add the underside thermal break.
  3. Mount the plywood hanger board to the frame's solid members; dry-fit hangers and confirm spacing/height-stagger or two-rank layout with the actual instruments (no strings attached to climate yet).
  4. Line walls with Spanish cedar; gasket and latch the door.
  5. Install the grommet and route the two low-voltage leads; mount the fans and the LED strip (channel behind the front top rail) with the driver/dimmer and door reed switch; set the monitor on the shelf and place the Boveda packs. Keep the manual diffuser on hand.
  6. Run empty for 1–2 weeks: off-gas, calibrate the sensor (salt test), and watch RH. Confirm Boveda + cedar hold ~45–50% on their own; learn how far it drifts in a dry spell so you know when to reach for the diffuser. (Only if RH persistently climbs past ~55% would you add a dehumidifier or the optional automated loop.)
  7. Only once it holds ±3% RH for several days, move the instruments in — least valuable first as a final check, vintage Rickenbacker last.
